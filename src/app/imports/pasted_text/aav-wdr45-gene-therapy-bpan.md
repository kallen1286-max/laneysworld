14 Abstract 300 words
15 Beta-propeller protein-associated neurodegeneration (BPAN) is an ultra-rare, X-linked dominant,
16 neurodegenerative disease caused by loss-of-function mutations in the WDR45 gene. It manifests in
17 neurodevelopmental delay and seizures followed by secondary neurologic decline with
18 dystonia/parkinsonism and dementia in adolescence and early adulthood and is characterized by
19 progressive accumulation of iron in the basal ganglia. WDR45 encodes β-propeller-shaped scaffold
20 protein, or WIPI4, which plays an important role in autophagosome formation. While the mechanisms of
21 how WIPI4 loss of function results in neurologic decline and brain pathology have not yet been
22 established, findings of lower autophagic activity provide a direct link between impaired autophagy and
23 neurologic disease in BPAN. Here we performed phenotypical characterization of a novel mouse model
24 of BPAN, Wdr45_ex9+1g>a mouse. We identified hyperactive behavior and reduction of autophagy
25 markers in brain tissue in Wdr45_ex9+1g>a hemizygous males as early as at 2 months of age. Given the
26 early onset and spectrum of neurologic symptoms such as hyper-arousal and attention deficits in human
27 patients, this model presents a disease-relevant phenotype and can be used in preclinical studies. We
28 used this mouse model for a proof-of-concept study to evaluate whether AAV-mediated CNS-targeted
29 gene transfer of WDR45 can provide therapeutic benefit and be considered a therapeutic paradigm for
30 BPAN. We observed successful expression of human WDR45 transcripts and WIPI4 protein in the brain
31 tissue, rescue of hyperactive behavior, and correction of autophagy markers in the brain tissue. This data
32 demonstrates that WDR45 gene transfer can be a promising therapeutic strategy for BPAN.

33 Introduction
34 Beta-propeller protein-associated neurodegeneration (BPAN, OMIM:300894), also known as a
35 neurodegenerative disease with brain iron accumulation type 5 (NBIA5) and static encephalopathy of
36 childhood with neurodegeneration (SENDA), is an ultra-rare, X-linked dominant, neurodegenerative
37 disease caused by pathogenic mutations in the WDR45 gene (1, 2). The clinical manifestations of BPAN
38 are highly heterogeneous between individuals, and the disease is significantly more prevalent in females,
39 with only a few male patients identified to date, likely due to higher severity and embryonic lethality in
40 the hemizygous state (3, 4). Individuals with BPAN are phenotypically diverse and may present with a
41 global severe neurologic disability, relatively preserved gross motor function but severely impaired
42 language development, or have an attenuated phenotype, demonstrating measurable language skills
43 and spared neurologic function (5). During early childhood, other symptoms include seizures and
44 cognitive impairment such as repetitive movements, bruxism, limited attention span, hyper-arousal,
45 restlessness, and abnormal behaviors. Later in life, starting in adolescence or young adulthood, the onset
46 of progressive movement disorder in the form of dystonia and Parkinsonism, and dementia has been
47 documented (5, 6, 7, 8). The main brain MRI findings are hypomyelination and thinning of the corpus
48 callosum, which can be seen in some but not all patients, and a more common progressive deposition of
49 iron in the basal ganglia (9). Brain pathology evaluation is limited to two published cases and includes
50 neuronal loss, predominantly cerebral atrophy, gliosis, axonal swellings, Alzheimer ’s-like Tau-tangles,
51 and iron deposits in the basal ganglia (6, 10). The complexity of the clinical manifestations and the rarity
52 of the disease contribute to the difficulty of obtaining a diagnosis. BPAN can be misdiagnosed as autism,
53 epilepsy, or other forms of NBIA (9). Currently, there are no approved or experimental therapies for
54 BPAN, and treatment options are limited to only symptom management.
55 WDR45 gene encodes the WD repeat domain phosphoinositide-interacting protein 4 (WIPI4), one of four
56 proteins of the PROPPIN (β-propellers that bind polyphosphoinositides), also known as WIPI (WD repeat

57 domain, phosphoinositide interacting) family. WIPI proteins function as membrane remodeling proteins
58 with a central role in autophagosome formation in autophagy, a metabolic process of recycling
59 intracellular materials via lysosomal degradation necessary for cell survival. The initiating step of the
60 autophagic process is the formation of the phosphoinositide-3 kinase class III (PI3KC3) complex I
61 composed of VPS34, VPS15, Beclin 1, and ATG14L, and the consequent production of
62 phosphatidylinositol 3-phosphate (PtdIns3P, or PI3P) (11, 12). PI3P has a dual function in
63 autophagophore formation. First, it controls the signaling of autophagy initiation, and second, it is used
64 for building phagophore membrane fragments in the ER subdomains called omegasomes (13, 14, 15, 16,
65 17). The four mammalian WIPI proteins, WIPI1-4, act as PI3P effectors that decode PI3P signals and
66 consequently enable autophagophore formation, working as scaffolds and attracting their protein
67 partners to the forming autophagosomal membrane. WIPI4 forms a complex with ATG2 that recruits
68 phospholipids from the endoplasmic reticulum to expand the autophagic membrane (18, 19, 20).
69 Mutations of the ATG2A binding sites in WIPI4 disrupt autophagic flux (21) and have been documented
70 in BPAN patients, where the severity of the disease significantly correlates with the inhibitory effect of
71 these mutations (22). Over 90 WDR45 variants associated with BPAN have been documented, with no
72 specific protein domain linked to the disease, most resulting in complete protein loss (3). While the
73 precise pathophysiological mechanisms of how loss of WDR45 results in neurologic decline and brain
74 pathology, including brain iron accumulation, have not yet been established, findings of lower
75 autophagic activity and accumulating autophagic vacuoles provide a direct link between impaired
76 autophagy and neurologic disease (2).

78 (25) and conditional, pan-neuronal (26), and dopaminergic neuron-specific Wdr45 knock-out model (27).

80 the progressive course of the disease, motor and congnitive deficits develop in these mouse models late

83 translational nonclinical studies since most human patients have neurodevelopmental phenotypes, and
84 early interventions would be required for the optimal therapeutic strategy in this disease. Therefore, in
85 this study, we have characterized another mouse model of BPAN, a Wdr45_ex9+1g>a mouse, available

88 early as at 2 months of age. Using this mouse model, we have performed proof-of-concept studies of
89 AAV-mediated WDR45 gene transfer to rescue neurologic dysfunction and autophagy deficits in BPAN.
90 Our data show that AAV-mediated expression of human WDR45 was efficacious to rescue disease
91 phenotype in BPAN mice, proving that this approach can be a promising therapeutic strategy for BPAN
92 and opening the door to translational development.

273 Most individuals with BPAN have already developed signs of neurologic disease, such as motor and
274 cognitive deficits and seizures, before their diagnosis (5), and therefore gene therapy will have to be
275 administered to the symptomatic patients. To address this in our preclinical proof-of-concept study, we
276 set out to evaluate whether brain-targeted AAV-mediated WDR45 gene transfer can rescue neurologic
277 dysfunction in BPAN mice after symptom onset. To achieve broad CNS transduction and transgene
278 expression in adult symptomatic mice, we designed an AAV-PHP.eB-WDR45 vector, since AAV-PHP.eB
279 capsid results in broad brain transduction after systemic administration in mice (32). Human WDR45
280 expression in this vector was driven by the synthetic promoter JeT, which is currently being used in a
281 clinical trial for giant axonal neuropathy (GAN) (30, 33).
282 Only male mice were selected for this experiment since female homozygous mice showed no robust
283 disease phenotype. AAV-PHP.eB-WDR45 (7e11 vg/mouse) was administered via tail-vein injection at 4
284 months of age, i.e, after the onset of hyperactive behavior in Hemi mice. 2 months later, the effect of the
285 WDR45 gene transfer was evaluated in the open field test (Fig. 2A), and no improvement of neurologic
286 function was found in AAV-PHP.eB-treated BPAN mice compared to saline-treated littermates at this time
287 (Fig. 2B). 5 months later, at 11 months of age, all the mice were re-tested in the open field, and
288 correction of the hyperactive behavior was observed in the Hemi PHP.eB-WDR45-treated group
289 compared to 6-month read-outs (Fig. 2B), showing the therapeutic effect of treatment at this later time
290 point. No adverse events or weight changes were observed in the treated animals (Fig. 2C). qRT-PCR
291 analysis of post-mortem brain tissue showed expression of human WDR45 mRNA transcripts with the
292 mean value of 1.25e5 copies/ug total RNA and 1.5 vector genome copies/cell (Fig. 2D). Western blot

346 Discussion
347 BPAN is an ultra-rare severe disease with early neurologic and cognitive presentation in the form of
348 delayed motor development, seizures, and autism-like symptoms and a progressive course leading to a
349 movement disorder and dementia in the second and third decades of life. It was first described in 2012
350 (1). With a growing community of patients worldwide, the unmet medical need in BPAN is vast, with no
351 therapies or interventional clinical trials registered at www.Clinicaltrials.gov. While the pathological
352 cascade in this disease is mainly associated with disruption of autophagy due to loss of function of the
353 autophagy scaffold protein WIPI4, the precise pathophysiological mechanisms are currently unknown.
354 Several mouse models have been developed and characterized to better understand the role of Wipi4 in
355 brain development and function. They include a constitutive Wdr45 knock-out model with a stop codon
356 in exon 6 (23), a constitutive knock-out model generated by crossing Wdr45 fl/Y mice with Zp3-Cre line
357 (24), and a constitutive Wdr45 knock-out TALEN mouse (25). Conditional models include a pan-neuronal
Wdr45 knock-out model (26) and a dopaminergic neuron-specific WDR45Fl/Fl/DATCreERT2 358 knock-out model
359 (27). These models showed progressive neuropathologic features in the brain, including microgliosis and
360 astrocytosis in some brain regions, axonal swelling and degeneration, loss of cerebellar Purkinje cells,
361 defective autophagy, and accumulation of p62/SQSTR aggregates with various ages of onset in different
362 models and the earliest signs reported at 4 months. Cognitive deficits (impaired spatial and contextual
363 memory), autism-spectrum disorders-like phenotype, and locomotor deficits have been detected in
364 BPAN mouse models at different ages: in full-body Wdr45 knock-out mice by Wan et al (23), both motor
365 and cognitive deficits were reported at 6-8 months, with the exception of the reduced marble burying
366 behavior and a higher sensitivity to pilocarpine-induced seizures observed in KO mice at 2 months; in the

367 TALEN Wdr45 KO model, statistically significant motor and cognitive deficits were observed after 11
months of age (23, 25). Both CNS-specific Wdr45 knock-out models, Nes-Wdr45Fl/Y 368 and
Wdr45Fl/Fl/DATCreERT2 369 mice, demonstrated the onset of disease phenotype after 11 and 17 months,
370 correspondingly (26, 27), suggesting milder phenotype in the conditional models. In addition to the
371 neurodegenerative phenotypes described earlier, Wipi4 was recently shown to play a role in brain
372 development (43). In mouse brain development, expression of Wipi4 was shown to be the highest at
373 embryonic development, dropping after post-natal day 8 and reaching the lowest level by 3 months of
374 age. Acute knock-down of Wdr45 led to abnormal dendritic development and synaptogenesis supporting
375 its role in brain development and further linking loss of WIPI4 function with neurodevelopmental
376 abnormalities seen in individuals with BPAN. Overall, despite the enthusiastic effort generating and
377 characterizing these mouse models, late or subtle phenotypes precluded them from being strong
378 disease-relevant models of BPAN that can be used for preclinical testing of drug candidates.

388 first report of the early phenotype in a BPAN mouse model directly relevant to human disease. The
389 observed hyperactivity readouts provide valuable disease-relevant outcome measures for preclinical

493 Conclusions
In conclusion, we performed characterization of the novel Wdr45 ex9+1g>a 494 mouse model for BPAN and
495 identified hyperactivity as a disease-relevant functional phenotype that can be used for preclinical
496 assessment of therapeutic interventions. On the molecular level, we found a reduced abundance of the
497 two autophagic markers in the brain tissue of BPAN mice, Lc3 and Wipi2, indicative of inhibition of
498 autophagosome formation. Importantly, unlike previously published models of BPAN, these phenotypical
499 characteristics developed in the BPAN mouse model early in the course of the disease. Given the early
500 onset of BPAN disease in human patients, this model is advantageous for preclinical trial design and
501 outcome interpretation in translational and therapy-focused studies. We used this mouse model for a
502 proof-of-concept preclinical study to evaluate whether AAV-mediated CNS-targeted gene transfer of
503 WDR45 can provide therapeutic benefit and be considered a therapeutic paradigm for BPAN. We
504 observed successful expression of human WDR45 transcripts and WIPI4 protein in the brain tissue,
505 rescue of hyperactive behavior, and correction of autophagy markers in the brain tissue. This data
506 demonstrates that WDR45 gene transfer can be a promising therapeutic strategy for BPAN if efficacious
507 expression levels can be achieved in the brain.
